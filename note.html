Halation: the flaw that
taught us to see.

MARCH 2025 · 8 MIN READ · THE DARKROOM

In the summer of 2021, a secondhand flatbed scanner chewed through a box of family photographs. Most came out streaked, banded, ruined. One came out better than the original — a sunset wrapped in a soft red glow, the kind of warmth no living printer seems able to make anymore.

That glow has a name. Halation — from the Greek for "the sun's edge." It happens when light pierces all the way through a film's emulsion, strikes the backing, and bounces back, blooming around the brightest points of the image. Film engineers spent decades trying to eliminate it. Photographers spent decades learning to love it.

A photograph without flaws is a sentence without a voice.

We couldn't stop asking the question that scanner forced on us: why did the error feel more honest than the photograph? The answer, as far as we can tell, is that perfection is information, but flaws are memory. The glow says: light traveled here. It bounced. It fought its way back to the surface. The image carries the story of its own making.

So we stopped trying to fix the glow and started bottling it. Three years of tuning — hundreds of test frames per recipe, named after the eras they remember. That's why every filter here is called a chemistry, and why the whole project carries the name of a beautiful mistake.

Bring us your almost-perfect frames. The darkroom light is on.
